## Use case

Artificial intelligence is increasingly used in educational and professional environments for research, writing, coding, and other activities. However, the relationship between AI usage and reported productivity, satisfaction, accuracy, time savings, and financial costs requires further investigation.

This project uses data cleaning and exploratory data analysis (EDA) to investigate AI usage patterns among students and professionals, identify relationships between key variables, and compare outcomes across different user groups.

The study also considers the potential relevance of AI adoption to architectural education and professional workflows in the built environment. Since the primary dataset includes multiple professions, findings are not assumed to represent the architecture sector specifically.

## Research questions

The project investigates eight research questions:

1. AI usage and outcomes: Are more daily AI-use hours associated with higher productivity scores, satisfaction, accuracy ratings, or time saved?
2. Students vs. professionals: Do students and professionals report different usage, time savings, costs, or ratings?
3. AI purpose and outcomes: Do people using AI for studying, coding, writing, or research report different productivity or satisfaction scores?
4. AI tools and ratings: Do reported outcomes vary by the AI tool used?
5. AI usage and cost: Are more AI-usage hours associated with higher monthly AI costs?
6. Income and AI cost: Does monthly AI cost vary with monthly income?
7. Ratings and recommendations: Are people with higher satisfaction, accuracy, or productivity scores more likely to recommend AI?
8. Background and usage: Do usage patterns differ by age, education level, profession, or country?

The final notebook will distinguish questions answered with statistical evidence from questions retained for further investigation.

## Limitations

* Self-reported information: Productivity, satisfaction, accuracy, and time savings may reflect individual perceptions.
* Correlation does not imply causation: Observed relationships do not establish causal effects.
* Sample representativeness: The dataset’s sampling process and population coverage require verification.
* Built-environment relevance: The primary dataset covers multiple professions, so general results cannot automatically be generalized to architecture or construction.
* Data quality: Missing values, formatting inconsistencies, outliers, and cleaning decisions may influence the results.
* Supporting datasets: Different data sources may not share compatible populations, locations, or time periods.
